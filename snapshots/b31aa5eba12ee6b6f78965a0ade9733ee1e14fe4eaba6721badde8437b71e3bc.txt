A Uniform Federal Rule Would Curb Gen AI Missteps In Court

In his 2023 Year-End Report on the Federal Judiciary, Chief Justice
John Roberts expressed optimism that artificial intelligence has the
"welcome potential to smooth out any mismatch between available
resources and urgent needs in our court system."[1]
AI can help lawyers practice more efficiently, such as by gathering
and summarizing information or creating initial drafts of documents.
And AI tools are already being used in self-represented litigations,
and have allowed pro se litigants — some of the most disadvantaged
users of the civil justice system — to win cases.[2]
Justice Roberts noted, however, that the "use of [generative] AI
requires caution,"[3] because the emerging technology is prone to
making mistakes — one of generative AI's "shortcoming[s] [is]
known as hallucination."[4]
As an IBM article explains, "AI hallucination is a phenomenon where
... a large language model" — a computer program that generates new information based
upon texts or other media it has analyzed — "perceives patterns that are nonexistent, ...
creating outputs that are nonsensical or altogether inaccurate."[5]
For instance, AI can generate fictitious cases and attribute fictitious language to real
cases.[6] Programs such as ChatGPT, Anthropic's Claude and Google's Gemini are all
powered by large language models and thus capable of hallucinating.[7]
French lawyer and researcher Damien Charlotin has identified over 670 cases where
generative AI produced hallucinated content, mostly in the U.S.[8] The New York
Times reported in November that Charlotin used to find "three or four examples in a month.
Now he often receives that many in a day."[9]
The same article said that Los Angeles-based lawyer Robert Freund, who also tracks legal AI
misuse globally, "has publicly flagged more than four dozen examples this year."[10]
Courts are beginning to take notice. In September, Chief U.S. District Judge Sheryl Lipman
of the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Tennessee wrote in Mid-America
Apartment Communities Inc. v. Philipson that "[t]he increased use by lawyers and non -
lawyers of artificial intelligence to draft legal documents has resulted in a scourge of
fictitious case citations in court documents in recent years."[11]
Similarly, in an October ruling in U.S. v. McGee, U.S. District Court Judge Terry Moorer Jr. of
the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Alabama described the problem of fake
AI-generated case citations as an "epidemic [that] continues unabated."[12]

Rules Suggestion 25-CV-S
The Guardian reported in November that courts as far away as Australia have complained
that judges must now serve as "human filters" for an "unsustainable" surge in AI-generated
content.[13]
Thus far, the burden of policing AI hallucination has mostly fallen on individual courts, which
have adopted a patchwork of rules and standing orders.[14] These provisions fall into five
broad categories:
• At one end of the spectrum, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth
Circuit has declined to adopt AI-specific rules, concluding that general prohibitions on
filing frivolous appeals suffice to deter attorneys' use of hallucinated authorities.[15]
• Many federal trial courts take a similar hands-off approach, but warn litigants about
the possibility of hallucination.[16] These warnings put litigants on notice of the risks
of using generative AI, making it easier for a court to sanction attorneys who rely on
hallucinated authority.
• Some courts allow litigants to use generative AI, but require counsel to disclose
whether generative AI — sometimes referred to as just "AI" — was used in a filing,
and to certify that any citations have been checked for accuracy.[17] Other courts
require litigants to identify the specific portions of documents that AI was used to
generate.[18]
• Some courts permit the use of AI, but limit the programs litigants may use.[19]
• Finally, some courts prohibit the use of generative AI.[20]
Laissez-faire approaches allow missteps to flourish and waste party and court resources
trying to locate false citations, identify other AI-based errors, and conduct sanctions
proceedings.[21]
Conversely, blanket bans on the use of generative AI throw the baby out with the
bathwater, eliminating the risk of hallucination but depriving litigants of the technology's
potential benefits. Pro se parties who may depend on AI tools may be disproportion ately
harmed.
As New York University School of Law professor Stephen Gillers told Bloomberg Law in
October, "The judge who bars use of AI under any circumstance is misguided."[22] Instead,
Gillers said, "[w]hat the judge should say is learn how to use AI, [and] use it carefully."[23]
There are also problems with approaches taken by courts that have tried to strike a balance
between innovation and deterrence by adopting certain disclosure requirements and rules
limiting the applications litigants may use.
First, requiring a litigant to disclose whether AI was used does not actually prevent
hallucinations.
Further, requiring disclosure of the use of AI risks stigmatizing practitioners who make
responsible use of AI and may chill the use of tools that can be beneficial. That risk is hi gher
when the disclosure requirement applies to practitioners who use any sort of AI as opposed
to generative AI specifically.[24]
Rules Suggestion 25-CV-S
For these reasons, none of the strategies adopted by courts so far has achieved universal
acceptance. We propose a rule requiring litigants to certify that they have checked all AI -
generated citations and other content for accuracy.
As U.S. District Judge P. Kevin Castel of the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of
New York said in a 2023 ruling in Mata v. Avianca Inc., "there is nothing inherently improper
about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance," but lawyers retain "a
gatekeeping role ... to ensure the accuracy of their filings."[25]
The American Bar Association has likewise held that a lawyer's "duty to provide competent
representation" may require "an appropriate degree of independent verification or review"
of an AI tool's output.[26]
We propose an amendment to Rule 11(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure to require
the following certification in any document containing citations that is filed with a court:
I certify that I or an individual under my supervision has reviewed all content in this
filing created with the assistance of generative artificial intelligence to ensure that
the authorities cited therein exist, have been accurately cited, and support the
propositions for which they have been cited. I further acknowledge that the court
may take corrective action, including imposition of sanctions, if hallucinated content
is submitted to the court.
This language would put attorneys on notice as to their obligations and deter blind reliance
on generative AI, without deterring the use of potentially beneficial tools. The proposed rule
would also remind judges of the pitfalls of AI and encourage best practices, potentially
allowing members of the judiciary to avoid embarrassing missteps too.
The proposed rule fits neatly in the framework of Rule 11(a).[27] Under that rule, lawyers
are required to sign papers they submit to a court and include several pieces of information
in their signature blocks. Adding a certification below the attorney's signature line would not
be burdensome, and it would remind attorneys of their obligation to use AI responsibly
when submitting a motion or brief.
Moreover, Rule 11(a) offers a built-in remedy for failure to include the information it
demands in the form of a requirement that courts strike the offending filing with leave to
refile. This mandatory — but light and easily resolvable — sanction complements and
effectuates the proposed rule's purpose of ensuring that attorneys pause to consider
whether they have used AI responsibly.
As reported in Bloomberg Law on Dec. 1, U.S. Magistrate Judge Allison Goddard of the U.S.
District Court for the Southern District of California counsels, "The best approach is to run
toward technology, not away from it," and that goes for generative AI, as well.[28]
But running toward technology does not mean running blindly. A rule would help address
the recent and growing number of missteps by lawyers as a result of the use of generative
AI in court filings.
We encourage the federal judiciary's Advisory Committee on Civil Rules to consider the
proposed rule or a similar federal court rule to replace the current patchwork of orders.
As U.S. District Judge Marina Garcia Marmolejo of the U.S. District Court for the Southern
District of Texas said in a November article in Ars Technica, courts have "scant resources to

A uniform federal rule would also provide a model for the states. Our proposed rule would
provide clarity to the bar, curb AI abuses and relieve judges from time spent policing fake
